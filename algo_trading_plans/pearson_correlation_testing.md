Note: VOO and NVDA are used as the example 2 stocks. The user / ML model should be able to choose 2 stocks and test them to see if they have a correlation. 

Phase 0 — Define What "Relationship" Actually Means Before You Test Anything
This is the step most people skip and it's why they get confused by conflicting results later. There are actually four different types of relationships two stocks can have and each one requires a different test:
Direction relationship — do they tend to go up and down on the same days? This is what Pearson correlation measures.
Magnitude relationship — when one moves 2%, does the other move a predictable amount too? This is what beta and linear regression measure.
Lead/lag relationship — does one stock tend to move before the other? NVDA might lead VOO by a day, or VOO might lead NVDA. This is what cross-correlation and Granger causality test.
Long-run relationship — even if they diverge day to day, do they always drift back together over weeks or months? This is what cointegration tests.
Before running a single calculation, write down which of these you're actually looking for. A pairs trader cares about cointegration. A regime filter cares about direction correlation. A lead/lag signal cares about cross-correlation. Knowing your goal stops you from testing everything and concluding nothing.

Phase 1 — Visual Inspection (Do This Before Any Math)
Never start with statistics. Start with your eyes. Plots will show you things that numbers hide — a structural break in 2022 where the relationship changed, a single crash day that's inflating your correlation, or a period where the two stocks completely decoupled.
The four plots you need, in order:
Plot 1 — Cumulative return chart of both stocks on the same axis. You want to see if they trend in the same direction over years, not just if they correlate on individual days. If NVDA is up 800% and VOO is up 80%, they clearly have different magnitudes even if they move together directionally.
Plot 2 — Scatter plot of daily returns. Put VOO returns on the X axis and NVDA returns on the Y axis, one dot per trading day. If the relationship is real and linear, you'll see an elliptical cloud pointing diagonally up-right. If you see a blob with no shape, the relationship is weak. If you see a banana curve, the relationship exists but isn't linear and Pearson will understate it.
Plot 3 — Rolling 60-day correlation over time. This is the most important visual. A single correlation number computed over 5 years tells you very little. The rolling version tells you when the relationship was strong, when it broke down, and whether it's currently strong or weak. If this line is all over the place between 0.2 and 0.9, the relationship is real but unstable.
Plot 4 — The spread between the two normalized prices. Normalize both to 100 at the start date, then plot their difference. If the spread keeps returning to zero, that suggests cointegration. If it drifts away and never comes back, they are trending apart structurally.
Only after looking at all four plots should you start running statistical tests. The plots tell you what kind of relationship to expect and which tests are worth running.

Phase 2 — Static Correlation Tests
Now you run the numbers. There are three correlation tests and they each tell you something different. Run all three, not just Pearson.
Test 1 — Pearson Correlation. This measures linear relationship on a scale of -1 to +1. Use daily returns, not prices. Compute it over the full period and note the number. For VOO and NVDA you will likely see something in the 0.55 to 0.75 range depending on the time window you use. Anything above 0.5 is a meaningful positive relationship. Anything above 0.7 is strong enough to trade on. Anything below 0.3 is noise.
Test 2 — Spearman Rank Correlation. This measures whether the ranking of returns moves together, not the actual values. If NVDA has the 3rd best day of the year whenever VOO has a strong day, Spearman catches that even if the magnitudes are completely different. Compare your Spearman result to your Pearson result. If they are close (within 0.1 of each other), the relationship is genuinely linear and Pearson is telling you the full story. If Spearman is significantly higher than Pearson, the relationship exists but is non-linear — Pearson was understating it.
Test 3 — Kendall's Tau. This is more robust to outliers than either Pearson or Spearman. Crash days and meme rallies can heavily distort Pearson because it squares the deviations. Kendall's Tau gives you a cleaner read of the underlying relationship without those extreme events dominating the result. If your Pearson is 0.65 but your Kendall's Tau is only 0.35, it means a small number of extreme co-movement days are driving most of your correlation number — the day-to-day relationship is much weaker than it looks.
At the end of Phase 2 you should have three numbers and a verdict: are all three tests pointing in the same direction and roughly agreeing on the strength? If yes, the relationship is real and robust. If they disagree badly, you have a non-linear or outlier-driven situation and need to dig deeper before trusting any single number.

Phase 3 — Statistical Significance Testing
Correlation numbers alone are not enough. A correlation of 0.4 computed on 30 data points is basically meaningless. The same 0.4 computed on 1,000 data points is highly significant. You need to test whether the correlation you found could plausibly have occurred by chance.
Step 1 — P-value from Pearson test. When you run the Pearson calculation using scipy's pearsonr function, it returns both the r value and a p-value. The p-value tells you the probability that you'd see a correlation this large if the two stocks were actually completely unrelated. You want p < 0.05 (less than 5% chance it's random). For VOO and NVDA over several years of daily data, you will almost certainly get p values near zero — meaning the correlation is statistically real, not random.
Step 2 — Bootstrap confidence interval. The p-value tells you the relationship is real but not how reliable the specific number is. Bootstrap testing randomly resamples your return pairs thousands of times, computes the correlation on each resample, and builds a distribution of possible correlation values. The result is a confidence interval — something like "the true correlation is between 0.58 and 0.72 with 95% confidence." A narrow interval means the number is stable. A wide interval like 0.2 to 0.8 means the estimate is unreliable and you should not trade on it.
Step 3 — Permutation test. This is the most honest test. Randomly shuffle one return series, breaking any temporal relationship between the two stocks, and compute correlation on the shuffled version. Do this 10,000 times. Plot the distribution of those shuffled correlations. Then mark where your actual correlation falls on that distribution. If your real correlation is way out in the tail of the distribution of random results, the relationship is real. If it falls comfortably within the distribution of shuffled results, you have nothing.

Phase 4 — Stability Testing (The Most Important Phase)
A correlation that was 0.7 five years ago but is 0.2 today is not useful for trading. You need to know whether the relationship is stable enough to rely on going forward. This is where most amateur analysis fails — they find a historical correlation and assume it still holds.
Step 1 — Rolling correlation over multiple window sizes. Compute the rolling Pearson correlation using three different window sizes: 20 days (one month), 60 days (one quarter), and 252 days (one year). Plot all three on the same chart. A stable relationship will show all three lines tracking each other closely. An unstable relationship will show the 20-day line swinging wildly while the 252-day line moves slowly — meaning the short-term correlation is unreliable even if the long-term average looks good.
Step 2 — Correlation stability score. Take the rolling 60-day correlation series and compute its standard deviation over the full period. A standard deviation below 0.10 means the correlation is very stable. Between 0.10 and 0.20 means moderately stable. Above 0.20 means the correlation is too volatile to rely on — it's there sometimes and gone other times, and you can't tell in advance which regime you're in.
Step 3 — Out-of-sample period test. Split your data into two halves: the first 60% (your "discovery" period) and the last 40% (your "validation" period). Compute correlation separately on each half. If the correlation in the validation period is within 0.15 of the discovery period, the relationship is reasonably stable across time. If the correlation drops by more than 0.20 from one period to the next, the relationship may be real but it's regime-dependent — it comes and goes based on market conditions.
Step 4 — Regime-specific stability. Break your data into bull market periods (SPY trending up), bear market periods (SPY trending down), and high volatility periods (VIX above 25). Compute correlation separately for each regime. This is critical for VOO and NVDA specifically because their correlation almost certainly spikes during crashes (everything becomes correlated when markets panic) and weakens during calm bull markets (NVDA runs on its own AI narrative). If correlation only exists in crashes, it's not tradeable in normal conditions.

Phase 5 — Lead/Lag Analysis
This tests whether one stock tends to move before the other. A lead/lag relationship is potentially more useful than a same-day correlation because it gives you advance warning.
Step 1 — Cross-correlation at multiple lags. Compute the correlation between NVDA today and VOO at various offsets: VOO yesterday (lag -1), VOO two days ago (lag -2), VOO tomorrow (lag +1), and so on, typically from -5 days to +5 days. If the correlation is highest at lag 0 (same day), there is no lead/lag. If it peaks at lag +1 (correlation is highest when you compare today's NVDA to tomorrow's VOO), then NVDA leads VOO by one day.
Step 2 — Granger causality test. This is a formal statistical test for lead/lag. It asks: does knowing yesterday's NVDA return help predict today's VOO return, beyond what you'd predict from yesterday's VOO alone? If yes, NVDA "Granger-causes" VOO. Run the test in both directions. For VOO and NVDA specifically, you will likely find that SPY/VOO moves Granger-cause NVDA (the broad market pulls NVDA along) but not strongly the other way — a single stock rarely drives an index ETF.
Step 3 — Practical significance check. Even if you find a statistically significant lead/lag, compute how large the predictive edge actually is. A stock that leads by one day but only explains 2% of the next day's variance gives you almost nothing tradeable after costs. The lead needs to explain at least 5-10% of variance to be worth acting on.

Phase 6 — Cointegration Testing
This is different from correlation. Cointegration asks whether two stocks share a common long-run trend — even if they diverge short-term, do they always come back together? This is the foundation of pairs trading.
Step 1 — Engle-Granger cointegration test. This tests whether the spread between two stocks (after adjusting for their relative scale using the hedge ratio from Phase 7) is stationary — meaning it bounces around a stable mean rather than drifting away. The test gives you a p-value. Below 0.05 means they are cointegrated. Above 0.05 means they are not cointegrated and pairs trading between them is dangerous.
Step 2 — Johansen cointegration test. A more powerful version of the same idea that handles both stocks symmetrically (Engle-Granger picks one as the "dependent" variable, which is arbitrary). If Johansen confirms what Engle-Granger found, the result is robust. If they disagree, treat the pair as not cointegrated.
Step 3 — Half-life of mean reversion. If the pair is cointegrated, compute how long it typically takes for the spread to revert halfway back to its mean after diverging. This is called the half-life. A half-life of 3-7 days is ideal for trading — fast enough to be actionable. A half-life of 60+ days means you'd have to hold positions for months to capture the reversion, which is impractical. For VOO and NVDA, expect a longer half-life because NVDA can diverge dramatically from the index during earnings and AI news cycles.

Phase 7 — Compute the Hedge Ratio
If all previous phases suggest the relationship is real, stable, and cointegrated, the final step before any trading logic is computing the correct hedge ratio. This tells you how many dollars of VOO to hold against each dollar of NVDA so that the combined position is market-neutral.
Step 1 — OLS regression. Regress NVDA prices on VOO prices. The slope coefficient (β) is your hedge ratio. For every 1 share of NVDA you hold, you hold β shares of VOO on the other side.
Step 2 — Rolling hedge ratio. The static hedge ratio from Step 1 is an average over your entire history. In practice the ratio changes over time. Compute it on a rolling 60-day window and plot it. If the hedge ratio is relatively stable (e.g. oscillating between 0.8 and 1.2), you can use a slowly-updating rolling value. If it swings wildly (e.g. between 0.3 and 3.0), the pair's relative scaling is unstable and the hedge ratio will be stale by the time you trade on it.
Step 3 — Validate the hedge ratio with residuals. After applying the hedge ratio, compute the residual spread (NVDA price minus β times VOO price). Plot this residual over time. If the hedge ratio is correct, the residual should look stationary — bouncing around zero without any visible trend or drift. If the residual is clearly trending upward or downward, the hedge ratio is wrong and needs recalibration.
